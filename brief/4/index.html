Before the Dec. 8 vote

San José data center rules would charge $50,000 per megawatt

The rules would apply to data centers drawing 20 megawatts or more, and add two fees on top of the taxes staff expect them to pay.

By J Gonzales · Nov. 22, 2026

How to weigh in: There is no Council meeting Tuesday, Nov. 24. The next one is Tuesday, Dec. 1, 1:30 p.m., at City Hall or by Zoom. Email city.clerk@sanjoseca.gov by 5 p.m. Monday, Nov. 30, with the item number in the subject line, or use eComment on the Legistar calendar (agenda, p. 4). The data center rules come up Tuesday, Dec. 8.

What matters this week

  • Deciding (Dec. 8): Whether to adopt citywide standards for data centers and research facilities that draw 20 megawatts or more, plus "related fee actions" (Planning Commission staff report, pp. 1, 16).
  • Cost to developers: A new Energy Reliability Fee, "initially at $50,000 per megawatt," and the City's Commercial Linkage Fee for affordable housing, at $5.20 per square foot (p. 10).
  • For residents: Staff write that "data centers are expected to generate $3.5 to $7 million annually" in utility user tax and property tax revenue, without saying whether that is per project or for all data centers combined (p. 5). The rules ban open-loop cooling that uses drinking water and require 100 percent renewable electricity (pp. 8–9).

Two new charges, and who gets the money

The Energy Reliability Fee would reimburse San José Clean Energy, the City's power provider, for the cost of securing enough generating capacity to meet peak demand. Staff say data center growth raises that obligation "before those customers begin paying SJCE rates." The fee would also pay for home batteries, smart thermostats, heat pumps and similar equipment that can cut peak demand. Half is due before the first building permit and half before occupancy. For three years, 35% of each project's fee would be reserved for the Council district where it is built (p. 10).

The Commercial Linkage Fee already applies to other commercial buildings; the City's 2020 study of that fee did not cover data centers. Staff give the example of $2.6 million for a 500,000-square-foot data center (p. 10).

Staff's illustration, which suggests the range may be per project: a single 99-megawatt data center would bring in about $7 million a year in utility user and property taxes, "plus approximately $20+ million in one-time revenue" from construction taxes and the two fees (p. 5). Staff also want sales and use tax on servers, cooling systems and generators credited to San José, which "could generate significant ongoing General Fund revenue" (p. 11).

What the City can't charge: staff say fees for fire response, schools and community facilities would need a separate study showing the project causes those costs. Instead, the policy points to voluntary and negotiated community benefits (p. 16).

When the rules would apply

The staff report, dated Nov. 6, sets a Dec. 8 first vote, a Dec. 15 second vote, and an effective date 60 days later, "tentatively February 14, 2027" (p. 16). The rules would cover any project not approved by then (p. 7).

On Nov. 17, the Council approved a proposal from the Mayor and four councilmembers to pause approvals for "data centers or large energy use projects" and bring back an "urgency ordinance" on Dec. 8 so the rules take effect "immediately upon adoption" (memo, p. 1; draft minutes, item 8.1). If the Council adopts the urgency ordinance Dec. 8, the rules take effect immediately and the February date would no longer apply.

The Planning Commission reviewed the rules Nov. 18 (agenda, item 5.a). As of Nov. 20, the City has not posted the commission's recommendation; we will add it when it does.

J Gonzales's take

[DRAFT — for Jay to rewrite or approve. Opinion.] The City has put a price on the extra strain data centers put on the power supply: $50,000 per megawatt, with 35% held for the host Council district for three years. That is a fair start. But the revenue figures are staff estimates, not commitments. If the Council adopts these rules Dec. 8, it should require a yearly public report on what each project actually paid and how the district share was spent.
